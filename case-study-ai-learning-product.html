Running continuous research for an AI learning product as its sole researcher

To protect confidentiality, this case study does not name the product, and visuals use sample data. I'm happy to discuss more details on request.

Company Study.com · Contract
Role Sole UX Researcher
Scope Rounds 13–17, continuing
Timeline Sep 2026 – Present
Moderated Usability Testing Think-Aloud Protocol Structured Interviews Cumulative Personas Research Ops

Context

The product is an AI learning tool for working professionals. An AI trainer sits beside a real AI assistant and coaches learners while they write prompts for scenarios drawn from their own jobs.

Study.com's only other UX researcher left two days after I joined, so I took over the research program mid-stream, starting with round 13. The product was changing every round, which meant research had to keep pace with the build rather than run as one-off studies.

Research Questions

Each round answered three questions, in order of importance:

  1. Does the learning loop (practice, AI response, coaching feedback) actually help people learn?
  2. Do onboarding and the first tutorial prepare learners to use the product on their own?
  3. Does the course content feel relevant to learners' real work?

Approach

I ran research as a continuous cycle, so each round both found new issues and checked whether last round's fixes worked.

Every round re-tests the fixes from the round before.

  • Sessions: moderated remote sessions of about 90 minutes, with screen share and remote control. Learners thought aloud while they worked, then answered a structured interview with ratings.
  • Participants: recruited on purpose across the range of AI experience, from daily multi-tool users to people who had only used voice assistants. Many findings split along that line, so I reported splits as splits.
  • Cadence: a new round on each new build, with the script updated to target what changed.

The Research System I Built

As the only researcher on a product that changed every round, I needed a system that kept synthesis fast without losing rigor.

Timestamped note-taking sheet

I log each participant's path and every issue against the session time, so patterns are quick to spot and every quote traces back to a moment in the recording.

AI-assisted workflows

I use a Claude skill to draft each round's report from my notes, then review and revise it. I gathered the team's feedback on the report and updated the skill to match: prioritized recommendations now come first, durable findings second, and perishable UI details last — a format the team liked. I also built additional Claude skills for team update messages and participant recruiting, so routine work takes less of each round.

Prioritization with a success bar

Each recommendation is ranked P0 to P2 and paired with "what success looks like," so the next round can check whether a fix actually worked. Priority depends on two things: whether an issue has shown up in earlier rounds, and whether it blocked learners from completing the task.

Cumulative personas

Four persona cards — such as a "Pragmatic Operator" and an "Anxious Knowledge Worker" — are updated every round instead of rebuilt, with each new participant added as evidence.

Cross-round summary at a phase change

When the product moved into a new phase at round 17, I wrote a summary of rounds 11 to 16 so the team could see which issues had been resolved and which were still open before the new phase began.

Built-in rigor

Every report opens with a methods caveat (small samples, moderator help, what was not tested), marks findings that repeat earlier rounds, and lists protocol fixes for the next round.

Key Insights

Specific findings changed from round to round, but they kept falling into four themes. I tracked each theme across rounds, so the team could see which problems were solved and which kept coming back.

1. Onboarding that prepares learners

What we kept seeing

Learners arrived with very different AI backgrounds. First-time users didn't know where to type or what the course would teach; experienced users wanted to skip the basics.

What it led to

Ask about AI experience at setup, introduce each part of the interface step by step, and show the learning path before practice starts.

2. Trust in the AI trainer

What we kept seeing

Learners relied on the trainer only when it responded to what they were actually doing. Vague directions, replies to background talk, and doing a step for the learner all eroded that trust.

What it led to

Clear visual separation between the trainer and the AI tool, explicit guidance tied to the lesson goal, and hints that escalate instead of doing the work.

3. Getting comfortable with the learning loop quickly

What we kept seeing

Prior AI experience shaped how fast learners settled in. Daily AI users moved quickly; voice-assistant users expected to talk to the AI and needed more guidance to complete tasks.

What it led to

A tutorial that ends with "type to the AI here, talk to the trainer," and controls that are easy to tell apart.

4. Learning gains and return

What we kept seeing

Scenarios drawn from learners' own jobs were consistently the strongest value driver, and learners left with concrete prompt-writing habits. Self-ratings, though, ran ahead of what we observed.

What it led to

A "show me what you learned" check added to the protocol, alongside relevance and likelihood-to-return ratings.

Spotlights: From finding to fix

Making practice feel like real work

What we saw

Since early rounds, learners had found practice tasks too generic to connect to their own jobs. It was a known issue that hadn't been resolved.

What I did

I kept tracking it as a recurring finding, so it stayed a priority in each round's recommendations.

What changed

The team added a few background questions at setup and used them to tailor each practice task to the learner's role and industry.

What the retest showed

In my rounds, learners consistently rated their scenarios as highly relevant, and job-based practice became the product's strongest value driver.

Re-testing onboarding

What we saw

Learners weren't sure how to use the product even after onboarding.

What changed

The team turned part of onboarding into a hands-on tutorial unit, so learners practice using the tool before real lessons begin.

What the retest showed

Experienced AI users moved through it easily, but first-time users still needed more guidance, such as where to type. That led to the next round's recommendations: highlight each part of the interface and end the tutorial by saying where to type.

Impact

Research became part of how the team builds, not a separate deliverable.

Research Velocity

  • At least 3 changes shipped every round, prioritized by how often an issue recurred and whether it blocked task completion — then re-tested the following round.
  • Personas guided the redesign of the learning loop and onboarding.

Research Reach

  • A clear starting point for the new phase: the rounds 11–16 summary showed the team what was done and what still needed work.
  • A report format the team adopted: leading with prioritized recommendations made each round's findings faster to act on.
  • Research reached leadership: session highlights were shown at a company all-hands.

Reflection

With only two participants per round, no single round proves much. What made the findings trustworthy was the cycle itself: tracking which issues repeated across rounds, and checking whether each fix held up the next time. I also learned to recruit across experience levels on purpose, because the same screen can be easy for a daily AI user and a wall for a first-time one.

Stepped in as sole researcher mid-stream and built a research cycle that turned each round of testing into fixes the team shipped and re-tested the next round.
